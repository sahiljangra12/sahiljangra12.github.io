Research

Undergraduate thesis · Tata Institute of Social Sciences · supervised by Dr. Kamal Kumar Murari, Centre for Climate Change and Sustainability Studies

Planetary Boundary Transgressions and Divergent Socio-Economic Pathways

A Cross-National Quantitative Analysis

Read the full thesis →

Can a country make life better for its people without putting ever more pressure on the planet?

That is the whole question. Answering it took 184 countries and thirty-three years.

That's the whole question. To try to answer it I needed three things: a way to measure environmental pressure, a way to measure how people are doing, and a way to watch both change over thirty years.

A · Data

Thirty-three years, one row per country per year

I built a single dataset covering a 195-country sample from 1990 to 2023, pulling indicators from public international sources — environmental, economic and social. Countries with too many gaps were dropped, so the working sample is 179 to 184 countries depending on the period.

I then grouped the years into three periods, and averaged each country's indicators within each period. Averaging smooths out one-off shocks — a drought, a currency crash — so what's left is closer to a country's underlying situation.

B · The index

Turning six environmental measures into one number

Environmental pressure isn't a single thing you can look up. A country might burn a lot of fossil fuel but keep its forests; another might have clean air but strip its soil. So I built a composite measure — the Planetary Pressure Index (PPI) — that combines six indicators into one score from 0 to 100, where higher means more pressure on the environment.

Two of the six work backwards: more renewable energy and more forest cover mean less pressure, so I flipped their sign before combining. Each indicator was standardised — rescaled so that all six count equally, rather than whichever happens to have the biggest raw numbers — and then averaged. A country needed at least four of the six to get a score.

Renewable energy share
% of energy from renewable sources
↓ higher = less pressure

↓ higher = less pressure

↑ higher = more pressure
Energy use per person

↑ higher = more pressure
A composite index is a simplification, and I treat it as one. It's a way to compare countries on a common scale, not a physical measurement of planetary damage.

D · Grouping

Grouping countries that look alike

Rather than ranking 184 countries from best to worst, I grouped ones that showed similar combinations of environmental pressure and development. The method is called k-means clustering: you tell the computer how many groups to look for, and it repeatedly sorts countries until each group is as internally similar as possible.

I grouped on five variables — the PPI score, GDP per capita, life expectancy, infant mortality and urbanisation — and settled on four groups. Four scored best on a standard quality check (the silhouette score, around 0.31 in every period) and, more importantly, the four groups made sense when I looked at which countries landed where.

E · Over time

Countries don't stay put

A single snapshot would have missed the interesting part. Because I ran the clustering separately in each of the three periods, I could track where each country started and where it ended up — and count how many moved between groups.

The headline movement is the transitioning group. It held 33 countries in 1990–2000 and 30 in 2000–2010 — then 85 in 2010–2023. Most of that growth came from countries that had been in the high-pressure, low-development group: 63 of them moved into the transitioning group between the second and third periods.

That's a real and large shift. What it means is a harder question: some of it is countries genuinely improving outcomes without adding pressure, and some of it is the boundaries between groups moving as the world as a whole changed. Mean PPI across all countries rose from 34.9 to 39.9 over the same three periods.

F · Associations

What moves with what

Finally I tested how environmental pressure lines up with each socio-economic outcome, one at a time, in each period. In plain terms: when PPI is higher, does life expectancy tend to be higher or lower, and by how much?

Higher pressure went together with higher income, longer life, lower infant mortality and more urbanisation — every one of those relationships was statistically significant in all three periods. Unemployment showed essentially no relationship at all. Female labour force participation ran the other way: higher pressure went with lower participation, consistently across all three periods.

The relationships also weakened over time. The correlation between PPI and life expectancy fell from 0.40 in the first period to 0.25 in the third — the link between putting pressure on the environment and living well is looser now than it was in the 1990s.

These are statistical associations, not proof that environmental pressure caused the observed outcome. Countries differ in hundreds of ways this analysis doesn't account for, and the relationship almost certainly runs in both directions.

Research Intern, Good Business Lab

2 June – 1 August 2025 · New Delhi, India

Good Business Lab is a labour research organisation that tests workplace interventions with randomised controlled trials. I worked on a study, run with the Automotive Component Manufacturers Association, on getting more women into automotive manufacturing jobs.

What a randomised trial is, briefly

If you simply ask women what they want in a job, you learn what they say. If you randomly offer different versions of the same job to different candidates and watch what happens, you learn what they do. That's the design: assign the variations at random, then compare.

The variations being tested

Gender ratio

Teams advertised as roughly 20% women versus roughly 50% women, to see whether who you'd be working alongside changes interest in the job.

Ergonomic support

Anti-fatigue mats and shoe insoles, to test whether physical comfort on the factory floor affects whether people stay.

Safety bundle

A named female HR contact and access to a grievance platform, to test whether these change job acceptance.

I contributed to the design, instruments and data collection for this trial. I'm not reporting its results: the analysis was ongoing when my internship ended, and any effect on hiring or retention is the study team's finding to publish, not mine to claim.

What I actually did

  • Built structured survey instruments in SurveyCTO from scratch, with logic checks, and tested them repeatedly on tablets before field use
  • Supported data collection, validation and quality checks on incoming field data
  • Helped train enumerators — walking through survey logic and why each question mattered
  • Built a two-phase automated CV generation system: first Google Forms and Apps Script, then SurveyCTO plus Python producing print-ready PDF CVs on site
  • Supported sampling by mapping residential clusters and industrial areas where eligible candidates could be reached
  • Ran a literature review on women's employment in manufacturing, and drafted a sustainability report summarising earlier pilot phases

Analysis work used STATA; automation used Python and Google Apps Script.
