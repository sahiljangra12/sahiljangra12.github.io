06 / SOCIAL SHORTFALL & CLIMATE OVERSHOOT

Working paper · Johns Hopkins · 1990–2022

Can basic needs be met within the climate boundary?

A country-year panel comparing four social thresholds (life expectancy, under-5 mortality, electricity access and employment) with consumption-based CO2, measured against a fair-share boundary of 1.61 t per person per year.

Open dashboard →
PAPERRead the PDF ↗CODEGitHub repository ↗RESULTSInteractive dashboard ↗
  • No country with consumption-based estimates met all four social thresholds within the boundary in any year.
  • The pooled-trend carbon requirement fell from about 26 t (1991) to 8.5 t (2022), still 5.3 times the boundary.
  • Absolute decoupling spread to 48 countries in 2011–2022, but only 2 of 41 decouplers above the boundary cut fast enough to reach it by 2050.

Python · Panel data · Logistic models · Decoupling analysis · Descriptive associations, not causal effects

09 / ENERGY TRANSITION & DEVELOPMENT

Research · 2000–2021

Energy Transition and Development

How has renewable energy grown across the globe since 2000, and how does it relate to national income? A tested Python pipeline from raw World Bank and UN SDG 7.2.1 data to results, figures and an interactive dashboard.

Open dashboard →
PAPERTechnical report ↗CODEGitHub repository ↗RESULTSInteractive dashboard ↗
  • Holding the same 187 countries fixed, the median renewable share of electricity rose from 10.3% in 2000 to 22.0% in 2021.
  • For all final energy, the renewable share falls steeply with income (ρ = −0.56); for electricity the link is weak (ρ = −0.17).
  • 74% of the 151 countries with more than 1 million people increased their renewable electricity share between 2011 and 2021.
  • Low-share countries are catching up, even after controlling for income and region.

03 / COMMENTARY

Theory

Commentary

Prediction, lived experience and whose goals a model serves.

Theory paper · October 2026

Knowing People Without Making Them

A Theory of Closure, Experience, and Person-Aligned Prediction in Complex Social Systems

Does a model understand people — or make them easier to predict? This paper distinguishes predictability discovered through information from predictability manufactured by shaping someone’s environment. It connects that distinction to lived experience, the exclusion of data-poor groups, and prediction directed toward a person’s own reflectively endorsed goals.

Read the full paper (PDF) ↗

The central ideas

World closureHow much uncertainty is resolved by variables a predictor controls.
The human residualExperience can explain differences that demographic models leave behind.
Data captureLess recorded data can mean less recognition of genuine need.
Person-aligned predictionSupport the person’s goals, with self-authored limits on steering.
Interactive evidence · S1 · S2 · S3
Explore the three simulations and supporting codeClosure ratchet, data capture and experience: question, setup, figure and reproducible Python

The paper makes three claims about prediction in social systems. Each simulation below is a small, controlled model built to make one claim visible: accuracy can be manufactured, missing data can hide need, and experience explains what demographics miss.

Illustrative simulations under stated assumptions, not empirical evidence about real people. The paper sets out nine falsifiable hypotheses for future testing.

S1 / Closure ratchet

When accuracy is manufactured

QUESTION
Can a recommender look more accurate simply by changing what people want?
SETUP
A simulated user interacts with three recommendation policies over repeated rounds: engagement-greedy, a neutral mirror, and goal-targeted (aims at the person’s stated goal).
WHAT THE FIGURE SHOWS
Predictability and entropy removed rise sharply under the greedy policy as preferences narrow; the mirror leaves preferences unchanged; goal-targeted moves choices toward the goal without the same collapse in diversity.
WHY IT MATTERS
High accuracy is not proof of understanding — it can come from shaping people. Evaluation should ask how predictability was achieved.

Open original figure (PDF) ↗

S2 / Data capture

Equal need, unequal visibility

QUESTION
Do people with fewer records get overlooked even when their need is the same?
SETUP
Groups with identical true need differ only in how many records exist about them. A standard shrinkage estimator pools sparse groups toward the average, and an eligibility cutoff decides who is served.
WHAT THE FIGURE SHOWS
As record count falls (log scale), estimated need is pulled below the cutoff and the probability of being served drops. A Monte Carlo check matches the closed-form result.
WHY IT MATTERS
Data-poor groups can be systematically under-served by “neutral” statistics — less data becomes less recognition.

Open original figure (PDF) ↗

S3 / Experience

What demographics leave out

QUESTION
How much of behaviour is left unexplained by income and group membership alone?
SETUP
A synthetic population in which risk-taking depends on demographics and on each person’s accumulated experience history.
WHAT THE FIGURE SHOWS
Adding experience raises in-sample explained variance from 0.27 to 0.54. People with the same income and group still differ, and larger experiential distance goes with larger differences in risk-taking.
WHY IT MATTERS
Models built only on demographic categories miss a “human residual”; lived experience is a measurable source of difference.
