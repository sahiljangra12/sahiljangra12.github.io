New Grid / Data Centers

CAUSAL STUDY · U.S. POWER GRID · EIA DATA

Grid / Data Centers

Where is data center load showing up on the U.S. grid, and what does it cost in carbon? A study of every hour of public grid data since 2019. Two zones of America’s largest grid have broken away from their neighbors: Ohio’s AEP zone now runs about 2.2 GW above a synthetic twin that tracked it within 1% for four years.

Open study →
ResultsThe twin test →ReportTechnical report ↗CodeGitHub repository ↗
  • Of 19 PJM zones, two sped up far more than the rest: AEP (Ohio) from −0.8% to +5.5% a year, Dominion (Virginia) from +3.6% to +7.2%.
  • In PJM, one more MWh of demand adds 0.42 t of CO₂, against 0.37 for the average MWh. Supplying the load that is new since 2023 emits about 10.9 Mt a year.
  • Dominion’s lead began before 2023, and the study says so: its AI-era speed-up is suggestive, not conclusive. Demand is seen by zone, not by customer.

2.1M hourly records · Synthetic control · Break tests · Marginal emissions · Refreshes weekly

Social shortfall & climate overshoot

WORKING PAPER · JOHNS HOPKINS · 1990–2022

Can basic needs be met within the climate boundary?

A country-year panel comparing four social thresholds (life expectancy, under-5 mortality, electricity access and employment) with consumption-based CO2, measured against a fair-share boundary of 1.61 t per person per year.

Open dashboard ↗
PaperRead the PDF ↗CodeGitHub repository ↗ResultsInteractive dashboard ↗
  • No country with consumption-based estimates met all four social thresholds within the boundary in any year.
  • The pooled-trend carbon requirement fell from about 26 t (1991) to 8.5 t (2022), still 5.3 times the boundary.
  • Absolute decoupling spread to 48 countries in 2011–2022, but only 2 of 41 decouplers above the boundary cut fast enough to reach it by 2050.

03 / COMMENTARY

Commentary

Prediction, lived experience and whose goals a model serves.

THEORY PAPER · OCTOBER 2026

Knowing People Without Making Them

A Theory of Closure, Experience, and Person-Aligned Prediction in Complex Social Systems

Does a model understand people — or make them easier to predict? This paper distinguishes predictability discovered through information from predictability manufactured by shaping someone’s environment. It connects that distinction to lived experience, the exclusion of data-poor groups, and prediction directed toward a person’s own reflectively endorsed goals.

Read the full paper (PDF) ↗
The central ideas
  • World closureHow much uncertainty is resolved by variables a predictor controls.
  • The human residualExperience can explain differences that demographic models leave behind.
  • Data captureLess recorded data can mean less recognition of genuine need.
  • Person-aligned predictionSupport the person’s goals, with self-authored limits on steering.
Interactive evidence · S1 · S2 · S3Explore the three simulations and supporting codeClosure ratchet, data capture and experience: question, setup, figure and reproducible Python＋

The paper makes three claims about prediction in social systems. Each simulation below is a small, controlled model built to make one claim visible: accuracy can be manufactured, missing data can hide need, and experience explains what demographics miss.

Illustrative simulations under stated assumptions, not empirical evidence about real people. The paper sets out nine falsifiable hypotheses for future testing.

S1 / Closure ratchet

When accuracy is manufactured

Question
Can a recommender look more accurate simply by changing what people want?
Setup
A simulated user interacts with three recommendation policies over repeated rounds: engagement-greedy, a neutral mirror, and goal-targeted (aims at the person’s stated goal).
What the figure shows
Predictability and entropy removed rise sharply under the greedy policy as preferences narrow; the mirror leaves preferences unchanged; goal-targeted moves choices toward the goal without the same collapse in diversity.
Why it matters
High accuracy is not proof of understanding — it can come from shaping people. Evaluation should ask how predictability was achieved.
S2 / Data capture

Equal need, unequal visibility

Question
Do people with fewer records get overlooked even when their need is the same?
Setup
Groups with identical true need differ only in how many records exist about them. A standard shrinkage estimator pools sparse groups toward the average, and an eligibility cutoff decides who is served.
What the figure shows
As record count falls (log scale), estimated need is pulled below the cutoff and the probability of being served drops. A Monte Carlo check matches the closed-form result.
Why it matters
Data-poor groups can be systematically under-served by “neutral” statistics — less data becomes less recognition.
S3 / Experience

What demographics leave out

Question
How much of behaviour is left unexplained by income and group membership alone?
Setup
A synthetic population in which risk-taking depends on demographics and on each person’s accumulated experience history.
What the figure shows
Adding experience raises in-sample explained variance from 0.27 to 0.54. People with the same income and group still differ, and larger experiential distance goes with larger differences in risk-taking.
Why it matters
Models built only on demographic categories miss a “human residual”; lived experience is a measurable source of difference.

RURAL / AUGUST–SEPTEMBER 2023

Osmanabad, Maharashtra

Climate, agriculture and household sustainability

What I investigated
How agricultural communities understand climate conditions, farming practices and adaptation, alongside the socioeconomic realities of farming households.
How I worked
I designed survey instruments and collected primary socioeconomic data directly from farming households. Field observations documented agronomy, household sustainability and community experiences of climate adaptation. The work brought household economics, agricultural practices and adaptation strategies into the same enquiry, rather than treating environmental conditions as separate from livelihoods.

01 / THE QUESTION

Development at what cost?

The thesis begins with a question: do countries with better living conditions also place more pressure on the planet?

02 / THE MEASUREMENT

Make countries comparable

The Planetary Pressure Index (PPI) is a constructed composite of six selected environmental-pressure indicators. Higher values mean more pressure relative to other countries in the sample. One common scale allows comparison across three periods from 1990 to 2023.

03 / THE ANALYSIS

Look for shared pathways

Countries are grouped into exploratory profiles using pressure, income, health, and urbanisation. Their movements and associations show patterns—not proof that one factor causes another.

DATA & METHOD

From environmental indicators to comparable pathways.

Planetary boundaries describe environmental limits within which human activity can operate. This framework motivates the study. The PPI is a researcher-constructed composite that summarises six selected environmental-pressure indicators; it is an exploratory, comparative proxy, not a direct measurement of the nine planetary boundaries or of whether a country has crossed a specific threshold. Its 0–100 scale is not the percentage of a boundary crossed.

The sample includes 182 countries drawn from UN member states and the State of Palestine, retaining countries with a mean population of at least 100,000. Annual observations from 1990–2023 are summarised into three periods: 1990–2000, 2001–2010 and 2011–2023. Period averages reveal broad patterns; they do not describe every change within a decade.

What goes into the Planetary Pressure Index?

Renewable energy share · reversed
A smaller renewable share contributes more pressure to the index.
Forest cover · reversed
Less forest cover contributes more pressure.
Agricultural land
A larger agricultural land share is coded as greater pressure on land systems.
Fertiliser use
Greater fertiliser use contributes more pressure through this agricultural input measure.
PM2.5 exposure
Higher exposure to fine particulate air pollution contributes more pressure.
Energy use per capita
More energy use per person contributes more pressure.
  1. Standardise and combine. Z-scores put indicators with different units onto a comparable scale. Their average forms the index when at least four of the six indicators are available. It is rescaled to a single 0–100 scale shared by every period, so the same value has a consistent meaning over time.
  2. Group comparable profiles. One k-means model is fitted to all country-periods together. It uses PPI, log GDP per capita, life expectancy, infant mortality and urbanisation to identify four broad profiles. Income is logged to reduce the influence of very large differences in its scale. The profiles are exploratory typologies: assignments depend on the chosen features, scaling and the K = 4 configuration, and are not natural categories.
  3. Track transitions. The same grouping model makes it possible to count movements between profiles. Each comparison includes only countries with a group assignment in both periods. A move records a change in model-assigned profile, not a verified gain or loss in sustainability.
  4. Examine associations. Separate bivariate ordinary least-squares regressions relate each outcome to PPI in each period. Correlations summarise the direction and strength of that relationship; they do not isolate causal effects.
FULL RESULTS

Development improves. Pressure does not disappear.

More developed profiles tend to have longer lives, higher incomes and greater urbanisation, alongside higher planetary pressure. The association weakens over time, but this alone is not evidence that development has become independent of environmental pressure.

INTERPRETATION & REPRODUCIBILITY

What this research can—and cannot—say.

The analysis identifies cross-country patterns. It does not show that environmental pressure causes better living conditions, or that reducing pressure necessarily lowers development. Bivariate models do not control for other influences, and period averages can conceal variation within countries and within each period. Eighteen regressions are reported without multiple-comparison adjustment, and statistical significance does not by itself indicate practical importance.

The PPI combines six indicators, with at least four required. Indicator coverage can differ across countries and periods. Its 0–100 range is relative to this sample, not a direct measure of safe environmental limits. A roughly flat country mean is not proof that total global environmental pressure is unchanged.

Social shortfall, climate overshoot and the falling carbon cost of development

Can countries meet the basic needs of their people while staying within a fair per-person share of the climate boundary? Explore the evidence country by country and year by year.

Paper ↗Code ↗Results ↓
0countries met all four social thresholds within the boundary in any year (consumption-based CO2)

The framework

Every country-year is judged on two criteria. Social foundation: life expectancy ≥ 74 years, under-5 mortality ≤ 25 per 1,000, electricity access ≥ 95% and employment ≥ 94% of the labour force. Climate boundary: consumption-based CO2 ≤ 1.61 t per person (equal share of the 2 °C budget). Together they give four configurations.

Shortfall, within boundaryBasic needs unmet, low emissions
Shortfall and overshootBasic needs unmet, emissions too high
Foundation met, overshootNeeds met at a climate cost
Safe and justNeeds met within the boundary, never reached

What the evidence shows

  1. No safe and just space. Among the 114 countries with consumption-based estimates, none met all four thresholds while emitting within the boundary. All 36 that crossed the social foundation did so while overshooting (30 of 30 under a three-year sustained definition).
  2. Accounting matters. With production-based emissions (155 countries), only Costa Rica and Sri Lanka appear safe and just, in 12 country-years, and both exceed the boundary once emissions embodied in imports are counted.
  3. Development is getting cheaper in carbon. The CO2 per person at which half of countries meet the foundation fell 3.6% a year, from ~26 t to 8.5 t; for the health thresholds alone it fell to 2.6 t.
  4. But not fast enough. Absolute decoupling spread from 24 countries (2001–2010) to 48 of 114 (2011–2022), yet the median cut among decouplers above the boundary was 1.6% a year against 5.5% required.
  5. Configurations are sticky. Most countries stayed in their configuration between periods, and no country moved into the safe and just space.

Methods in brief

A country-year panel (155 countries, population ≥ 1 million, 1990–2022) built from World Bank WDI, Our World in Data CO2 accounts and Maddison PPP income. Interior gaps up to three years are interpolated, never extrapolated. The carbon requirement is the 50% point of a yearly logistic fit of foundation attainment on log CO2 per person, with bootstrap confidence intervals. Decoupling compares trend growth of CO2 and GDP per person; the required rate is the constant annual cut that reaches 1.61 t by 2050.

Robustness

The level of the requirement depends on how the foundation is defined, but its decline and its position above the boundary hold in every specification.

One planetary boundary and four social indicators; equal-per-capita shares ignore historical responsibility; the requirement is a cross-country summary, not what any single country needs. All estimates are descriptive associations, not causal effects.

Income tells two different stories

Do New Street Trees Cool Baltimore?

A simple question, tested over time: when Baltimore plants new street trees, do the surrounding surfaces become cooler?

Within-neighborhood evidence from Baltimore's street-tree program, 2006–2025

The study, in plain language

Small trees. A measurable change.

The study estimates a small cooling effect that appears after planting and grows as trees mature. It compares blocks inside the same neighborhood, rather than assuming that every difference between leafy and less leafy places is caused by trees.

What was measuredSummer daytime surface temperature from satellites — the heat of surfaces, not the air people breathe.

Why this matters

The question

Cities plant trees to fight extreme heat. Baltimore set a goal in 2007 of doubling its tree canopy to 40% by 2037. But most evidence that trees cool cities compares places that already differ in many ways. Leafy neighborhoods are cooler, but they are also different in income, housing and history.

This study asks the question a city actually faces: how much cooling did new plantings buy, and when?

A closer comparison

How I answered it

In short: compare nearby blocks in the same year, use suitable empty tree sites as a comparison, and check whether cooling appears before or after the estimated planting time.

Three design choices separate the effect of trees from the reasons places got trees:

  1. A fair comparison group. The city's 2017–18 tree census also records empty planting sites it judged suitable. Those sites passed the same screening as planted ones but had no tree yet.
  2. Compare blocks within the same neighborhood and year. On a grid of 15,166 cells of 90 m, each block is compared only with other blocks in the same neighborhood in the same year. That removes citywide and neighborhood-wide changes.
  3. A built-in timing test. Trunk size tells roughly when a tree was planted. If trees cause the cooling, it should start after planting, not before.

Data: Baltimore Forestry Division tree inventory (193,009 records), Landsat 5/7/8/9 summer surface temperature (111 clear scenes, 2006–2025), and TreeBaltimore planting counts by neighborhood.

Why comparing whole neighborhoods misleads

If you compare whole neighborhoods, neighborhoods that later got more trees were already cooling before planting began (pre-trend test p < 0.001). A naive analysis would credit trees for that. Comparing blocks within the same neighborhood removes this, and the pre-planting test passes. For anyone evaluating urban greening, this is the main lesson: neighborhood-level comparisons pick up the reasons programs chose those places.

What to take away

What does this mean for a street?

Why is the cooling number so small?

It is an average over an entire 90 m block. A young tree shades only a small part of that area, so the average change is much smaller than the temperature difference between shade and sunlit pavement.

Does this prove that the air is cooler?

No. Satellites measure surface temperature in this study. The results do not directly measure air temperature, how hot a person feels, or health outcomes.

Does the before-planting test prove there was no change?

No. The pre-trend test (p = 0.63) did not detect a clear difference before planting. That supports the timing of the result, but it is not proof of exactly zero change or a guarantee that every source of bias is removed.

What should a reader be cautious about?

Planting dates are estimated from trunk size. Demolition may also affect cooling. The study checks alternative explanations, but these uncertainties remain important when interpreting the result.

What remains uncertain

Limitations

  • Planting dates are inferred from trunk size because the city's public data has no usable planting dates.
  • A placebo test on sites judged unplantable also showed cooling, likely from vacant-house demolitions in rowhouse blocks. Controlling for this leaves the main result unchanged, but demolition records should be added directly.
  • The outcome is daytime surface temperature, not air temperature or health.
