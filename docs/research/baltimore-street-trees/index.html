Do New Street Trees Cool Baltimore?

Do New Street Trees Cool Baltimore?

A simple question, tested over time: when Baltimore plants new street trees, do the surrounding surfaces become cooler?

Within-neighborhood evidence from Baltimore's street-tree program, 2006–2025

Sahil · MSE in Data Science, Johns Hopkins University · October 2026

Small trees. A measurable change.

The study estimates a small cooling effect that appears after planting and grows as trees mature. It compares blocks inside the same neighborhood, rather than assuming that every difference between leafy and less leafy places is caused by trees.

The question

Cities plant trees to fight extreme heat. Baltimore set a goal in 2007 of doubling its tree canopy to 40% by 2037. But most evidence that trees cool cities compares places that already differ in many ways. Leafy neighborhoods are cooler, but they are also different in income, housing and history.

This study asks the question a city actually faces: how much cooling did new plantings buy, and when?

How I answered it

In short: compare nearby blocks in the same year, use suitable empty tree sites as a comparison, and check whether cooling appears before or after the estimated planting time.

Three design choices separate the effect of trees from the reasons places got trees:

  • A fair comparison group. The city's 2017–18 tree census also records empty planting sites it judged suitable. Those sites passed the same screening as planted ones but had no tree yet.
  • Compare blocks within the same neighborhood and year. On a grid of 15,166 cells of 90 m, each block is compared only with other blocks in the same neighborhood in the same year. That removes citywide and neighborhood-wide changes.
  • A built-in timing test. Trunk size tells roughly when a tree was planted. If trees cause the cooling, it should start after planting, not before.

Data: Baltimore Forestry Division tree inventory (193,009 records), Landsat 5/7/8/9 summer surface temperature (111 clear scenes, 2006–2025), and TreeBaltimore planting counts by neighborhood.

Young street trees (trunk ≤ 1.5 in, planted roughly 2014–17). Each point is the change in a 90 m block's summer surface temperature per tree, relative to 2006–09. Bars are 95% confidence intervals.

  • Young trees cooled their blocks only after they were planted, and the effect grew as they matured. By 2022–25, each young tree had lowered its block's summer surface temperature by 0.026 °C. A block with five new saplings is about 0.13 °C cooler.
  • The size makes physical sense. A young crown shades about 0.1–0.25% of a 90 m block, and shade is 10–15 °C cooler than sunlit pavement. That predicts roughly 0.01–0.04 °C per tree.
  • The result holds up. It holds across six alternative analyses and a formal test showing that a hidden confounder three times as strong as mature-tree density would only move it to 0.024 °C.

Why comparing whole neighborhoods misleads

If you compare whole neighborhoods, neighborhoods that later got more trees were already cooling before planting began (pre-trend test p < 0.001). A naive analysis would credit trees for that. Comparing blocks within the same neighborhood removes this, and the pre-planting test passes. For anyone evaluating urban greening, this is the main lesson: neighborhood-level comparisons pick up the reasons programs chose those places.

What does this mean for a street?

Why is the cooling number so small?

It is an average over an entire 90 m block. A young tree shades only a small part of that area, so the average change is much smaller than the temperature difference between shade and sunlit pavement.

Does this prove that the air is cooler?

No. Satellites measure surface temperature in this study. The results do not directly measure air temperature, how hot a person feels, or health outcomes.

Does the before-planting test prove there was no change?

No. The pre-trend test (p = 0.63) did not detect a clear difference before planting. That supports the timing of the result, but it is not proof of exactly zero change or a guarantee that every source of bias is removed.

What should a reader be cautious about?

Planting dates are estimated from trunk size. Demolition may also affect cooling. The study checks alternative explanations, but these uncertainties remain important when interpreting the result.

Limitations

  • Planting dates are inferred from trunk size because the city's public data has no usable planting dates.
  • A placebo test on sites judged unplantable also showed cooling, likely from vacant-house demolitions in rowhouse blocks. Controlling for this leaves the main result unchanged, but demolition records should be added directly.
  • The outcome is daytime surface temperature, not air temperature or health.
